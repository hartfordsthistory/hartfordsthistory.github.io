Pub Note	"Rare official assessor's index map of San Francisco, uniquely mounted on hinged redwood boards for heavy office use. Compiled by Louis F. Holtz during his tenure as City and County Assessor, this highly detailed 1884 lithograph records the sprawling, rapidly developing street grid of late nineteenth-century San Francisco. Lithographed by the prominent local firm of A.L. Bancroft & Company, the map was designed as a master index to the city's municipal blocks, land tracts, and tax assessments. Presenting the entirety of the city's northern peninsula from the Presidio and Golden Gate Park south past Islais Creek, it captures San Francisco at a moment of intense urbanization, as old Mexican land grants and early homestead associations were increasingly rationalized into the municipal tax grid. The present example is a remarkable survival, mounted in redwood and hinged to fold for ease of storage, with evidence of some early redlining. An Essential Municipal Tool Produced specifically for the Assessor's Office, the index map functioned as a primary reference tool for city officials, title searchers, and real estate developers navigating the complex, often litigious landscape of San Francisco property ownership. Each subdivided city block is clearly assigned a number, corresponding to the detailed block books maintained by the assessor to track ownership, improvements, and tax liabilities. The sheer density of numbering across the northern and eastern portions of the city contrasts starkly with the vast, still-undivided tracts in the south and west, providing a precise snapshot of the frontier of urban development in 1884. A.L. Bancroft & Company, a premier publisher and lithographer on the West Coast during this era, executed the map with precision, ensuring its reliability as a foundational civic document. A Unique Working Artifact This specific example survives as a compelling artifact of municipal administration, having been bisected down its center and mounted onto thick, hinged redwood boards. This robust, utilitarian format allowed the large map to be folded closed like a ledger and stored compactly yet withstand the daily wear and tear of a busy land office or surveyor's desk. The heavy liquid staining, generalized toning, and surface wear across the paper bear testament to its active working life over decades of consultation. Such institutional tools were fundamentally ephemeral, used exhaustively until superseded by updated surveys and subsequently discarded, making the survival of this working copy—especially in its original bespoke wooden binding—a notable exception. Tracking the Expanding City The map offers a remarkably granular view of San Francisco's evolving landscape. The downtown core and neighborhoods south of Market Street are meticulously subdivided and numbered. Along the eastern waterfront, the "South Beach Water Lot Survey" and expanding infrastructure around "Central Basin" and "China Basin" reflect the city's booming maritime and industrial sectors. Further west, the map details the massive "Government Reserve" at the Presidio and the newly established "Golden Gate Park," which had only recently begun its transformation from shifting sand dunes into a cultivated urban oasis. Just east of the park lies the extensive complex of Victorian cemeteries—Laurel Hill, Calvary, Odd Fellows, and Masonic—surrounding Lone Mountain. Because these burial grounds would eventually be mandated to relocate to Colma in the early twentieth century to free up prime real estate, this map serves as an important record of their original configurations and immense footprint. To the south, the enduring legacy of Mexican land grants is evident in the vast, unsubdivided expanse of the "San Miguel Rancho," a stark contrast to the encroaching grid of streets. The southern frontier of the city is dotted with developing tracts and "Homesteads," including the "Noe Garden Reserve" (labeled "Pioche and Robinson's") and the complex topography around Islais Creek and Precita Place. The careful delineation of these peripheral areas highlights the assessor's mandate to catalog every parcel of land, no matter how remote or topographically challenging, as the city aggressively expanded its tax base outward. Rarity Apparently printed only for official use, the map is extremely rare. The Huntington Library holds the only recorded example." (Ruderman, 2026)

Summary	The **“Index Map of the City of San Francisco. Assessor’s Office 1884,”** compiled by Louis F. Holtz during his tenure as City and County Assessor and lithographed by A.L. Bancroft & Co., constitutes a rare and detailed municipal artifact from San Francisco’s late nineteenth-century period of rapid urbanization. Published as a wall map with a scale of 1:14,000 and measuring 64 by 76 centimeters, this official assessor's map was designed to serve as the master index for streets, blocks, land tracts, and tax assessments within the city. <br><br>The map captures the entirety of San Francisco’s northern peninsula, from the Presidio and Golden Gate Park in the west to Islais Creek in the south, chronicling its evolving urban landscape at a time when the city was expanding aggressively and rationalizing its earlier Mexican land grants and homestead associations into a municipal grid for property and tax administration. Each city block is assigned a number, corresponding to detailed assessor’s block books for ownership, improvements, and tax liabilities.<br><br>Key urban features include a dense, numbered grid in the downtown and eastern areas, contrasted with vast, unsubdivided tracts in the south and west, including the enduring Mexican “San Miguel Rancho.” The map also delineates prominent historic sites: the Presidio “Government Reserve,” the emerging Golden Gate Park, significant Victorian cemeteries (Laurel Hill, Calvary, Odd Fellows, and Masonic) surrounding Lone Mountain, the South Beach Water Lot Survey, Central Basin, and China Basin. These details offer a granular view of the period’s maritime, industrial, and residential development, as well as the interplay between planned development and the remaining pastoral or undeveloped land.<br><br>The functional design of this particular surviving example is notable. The map was mounted on thick, hinged redwood boards, allowing it to be folded closed and stored compactly—a necessity for frequent reference in the bustling assessor’s office. The physical object bears extensive signs of use, including staining, toning, and surface wear, testifying to its long working life and the ephemeral nature of such administrative tools. The map’s survival, especially in its original bespoke wooden format, is exceptional given most were discarded and superseded.<br><br>The map was evidently intended strictly for governmental use and is now extremely rare. It is reported that The Huntington Library holds the only other recorded example. As such, this piece serves both as a pivotal civic document and as a vital historical record of San Francisco’s urban form, land administration, and social geography in 1884.
